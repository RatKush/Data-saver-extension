Evaluation

A 30-day pilot, measured by you

The pilot exists because we will not quote you a saving we cannot measure. You deploy to a slice of your fleet, your own monitoring produces the number, and you decide what it is worth.

Why you measure instead of us

An extension can count the requests it blocked — that number is exact, and the product shows it. What it cannot do is know how many bytes those requests would have transferred, because Chrome exposes no such API to extensions in a production build. Every byte figure the product displays is labelled an estimate for that reason.

Your netflow, proxy logs or carrier reporting measure real egress. That is a better instrument than anything we could build, and it is already deployed.

What you need

  • 20–100 managed devices running Chrome or Edge. Enough that individual behaviour averages out; small enough to roll back in an afternoon.
  • A way to deploy an extension by policy — Group Policy, Intune, Jamf, or the Google Admin console. See the deployment guide.
  • Existing bandwidth reporting that can be filtered to those devices: netflow, a proxy, a firewall report, or per-line carrier data.
  • A named contact on your side who can answer whether a site broke, because that matters as much as the byte count.

How it runs

  1. Agree the measure and the threshold — before anything is installed

    Pick one metric, usually web egress per device per working day. Write down the number that would make this worth buying. Deciding afterwards is how pilots turn into arguments.

  2. Baseline for two weeks

    No deployment yet. Record the metric across the pilot group so there is something to compare against, over a period long enough to include a normal working pattern.

  3. Deploy by policy

    Force-install the extension and push a policy file. We supply one tailored to your environment — typically ads and images blocked, video left alone, and any internal applications exempted so nothing breaks on day one.

  4. Run for four weeks

    Collect the same metric. Keep a note of anything users report as broken; the per-site rules exist precisely to fix those without weakening the policy everywhere.

  5. Compare, and decide

    Against the threshold agreed in step one. If it is not met, that is a real answer and the pilot cost you a policy push and a rollback.

What we provide

  • The packaged extension, identical to the public Chrome Web Store build.
  • A policy file written for your environment, including exemptions for internal applications.
  • The deployment guide and direct help if a push does not apply cleanly.
  • A rollback procedure, agreed before you start.

Confounders worth naming up front

Web browsing is only part of your egress. Video conferencing, software updates, backups and cloud sync are untouched by this and can easily dominate a total. Filter to web traffic if your tooling allows it; if it does not, expect the headline reduction to look smaller than the effect on browsing itself.

Seasonality and population drift. A baseline over a holiday period, or a pilot group that changes size, will move the number for reasons that have nothing to do with the extension.

Video platforms ship unblocked by default. Streaming sites are exempt out of the box because blocking them breaks them rather than saving anyone a surprise. If your goal is to suppress streaming specifically, that is a policy change to make deliberately at step three.
